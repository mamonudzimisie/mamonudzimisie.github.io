> It sounds dangerous, but nobody gets hurt. Except maybe your pencil.

If you've been solving Sudoku for years and the grids have started to feel the same, Killer Sudoku is a great next step. The rules of ordinary Sudoku stay exactly as they are, and one new rule is added – and that one rule makes you think in a completely different way.

## The rules

Killer Sudoku has two rules.

**The first one is old.** Fill in the digits 1 to 9 so that every row, every column and every 3×3 box contains each digit exactly once. Just like ordinary Sudoku.

**The second one is new.** The grid is divided into **cages** – areas outlined with a dashed line. Each cage has a number in its corner. The digits in a cage must **add up to that number**, and **no digit may repeat within a cage**.

A cage can have one cell or several, and it can cross the border of a box. Every cell of the grid belongs to exactly one cage.

![A 3×3 box divided into four cages with the sums 3, 15, 7 and 20, and the same box solved: 1 and 2 in the 3 cage, 6 and 9 in the 15 cage, 3 and 4 in the 7 cage, 8, 5 and 7 in the 20 cage](/blog/killer-sudoku-rules.webp)

In classic Killer Sudoku there isn't a single digit on the grid – only cages and sums. Easier versions, like the ones in our book, already have some **starting digits** filled in, so you have somewhere to begin.

## Where does the name come from?

"Killer" is the name the puzzle got in Britain, where *The Times* started printing it in 2005, at the height of the Sudoku craze. The hard versions with no starting digits can give even experienced solvers a real fight – hence the scary label. In Japan the same puzzle was known earlier as *samunamupure*, from the English *sum number place*.

Easy Killers really are easy, though. The maths never goes beyond adding up small numbers, and the fun is in noticing what a sum tells you.

## Three ways to make your first moves

### 1. Cages that give themselves away

Some sums can be made in only one way. Two cells adding up to **3** are always 1 and 2 – there's no other pair of digits with that sum. You don't know yet which digit goes in which cell, but you know that no other digit can go there.

There are more of these "sure things":

- two cells, sum **4**: always 1 and 3
- two cells, sum **16**: always 7 and 9
- two cells, sum **17**: always 8 and 9
- three cells, sum **6**: always 1, 2 and 3
- three cells, sum **7**: always 1, 2 and 4
- three cells, sum **23**: always 6, 8 and 9
- three cells, sum **24**: always 7, 8 and 9

These cages are the best place to start every puzzle.

### 2. The rule of 45

The digits 1 to 9 add up to **45**. Since every row, column and box contains each digit exactly once, each of them adds up to 45 too.

How do you use it? Find a box (or a row, or a column) where the cages lying entirely inside it cover everything except one cell. Add up the sums of those cages and take the total away from 45. What's left is the digit in the free cell.

Example: a box holds three cages with the sums 15, 12 and 14, and one cell is left outside them. 15 + 12 + 14 = 41, so that cell must be 45 − 41 = **4**. No guessing, and no checking rows.

### 3. The last cell of a cage

When a cage has just one empty cell left, you fill it in by subtraction: a cage of 20 that already holds 8 and 5 needs 20 − 8 − 5 = **7**. And after every digit you write in, the usual Sudoku methods come back – check the row, column and box that digit has just landed in.
